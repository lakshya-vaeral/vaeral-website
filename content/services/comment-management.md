sectionOneHeading: "What comment management is"
sectionOne: |
  Comment management is the work of reading, answering, routing and moderating the comments that collect under a brand's own posts and under other people's posts about it.

  It is not the same as social media management. Nobody here is selling you a content calendar or producing your posts. This is the layer underneath them — the part that arrives unpredictably, is read by everyone who scrolls past, and is usually nobody's actual job until it becomes a problem. A well-made post with an unanswered complaint sitting at the top of its comments performs worse than a modest post with a good reply.

  The scope covers your organic posts, the comment sections under paid campaigns, video platforms where comments persist for years, community threads, and the escalation path for the comments that are not really comments at all.
sectionTwoHeading: "How it works"
sectionTwo: |
  ### 1. Inventory the surfaces where comments accumulate

  Organic posts, the comment sections under every live ad, video platforms, and the threads about you that you do not control. Paid campaigns are the surface most often missed: a brand will spend heavily on distribution and leave the comments beneath it unread for weeks, which turns paid reach into paid amplification of an unanswered objection.

  ### 2. Set the moderation policy before you need it

  What gets answered, what gets hidden, what gets escalated and what is best left alone — written down and agreed while nobody is under pressure. Policies invented during an incident are inconsistent, and inconsistency is what people screenshot. The policy also settles who owns the decision at two in the morning.

  ### 3. Answer in the register of the thread

  A reply that reads as corporate boilerplate under a specific complaint does more damage than saying nothing, because every later reader sees both the complaint and the evasion. Answers name the actual issue, say what happens next, and move the detail to a channel where it can be resolved.

  ### 4. Route the comments that are not comment problems

  A refund request, a bug report, a safety concern or a legal threat needs a ticket and an owner, not a better-worded reply. Most brands discover during this step that their comment section has been operating as an undocumented support queue.

  ### 5. Report what the comment layer says about the product

  Recurring themes, response times, which threads escalated and why. The comments are the cheapest customer research a brand has, and they are usually thrown away after they are answered.
sectionThreeHeading: "Who it's for"
sectionThree: |
  **This is a fit if** comments arrive faster than your team can read them, your paid campaigns are collecting objections nobody is answering, or your audience comments in several languages and the replies currently depend on whoever happens to speak one.

  **This is not a fit if** you want the comment section to look uniformly positive. Hiding a comment does not hide it from the person who wrote it — the platforms deliberately still show authors their own comment — and the discovery that a brand quietly suppresses criticism becomes its own post, usually with a screenshot attached.

  We manage the comment layer; we do not manufacture it. And where the same complaint arrives every week, moderation becomes documentation rather than a fix: that is a product ticket wearing a reputation costume, and answering it more politely each time changes nothing.

faqs:
  - question: "Should we delete or hide negative comments?"
    answer: "Almost never. Hiding a comment leaves it visible to its author, who can see it has been suppressed and often says so publicly with a screenshot. Delete only what breaches the platform's rules — spam, abuse, doxxing, or content that endangers someone. A specific complaint answered well is an asset; the same complaint hidden is a liability waiting for someone to notice."
  - question: "How quickly do you respond?"
    answer: "Fast enough that the reply is still part of the conversation, which in practice means the same working day for ordinary comments and much sooner for anything spreading. The window matters more than the wording: a good answer on day three is read by almost nobody, because the thread has already been decided by then."
  - question: "Which platforms are covered?"
    answer: "The ones your audience actually uses, which we establish in the inventory step rather than assuming. Typically that means the major social platforms, video platforms where comments persist and keep being read, and the community threads where your category is discussed. Coverage is scoped explicitly so nothing is quietly excluded."
  - question: "Do you post comments that look like they come from customers?"
    answer: "No. Undisclosed comments written to appear as independent customers breach the platforms' own rules on authentic behaviour and are the single fastest way to turn a manageable reputation problem into a permanent one. Everything we post is attributable to the brand."
  - question: "Can this be automated?"
    answer: "Partly, and only the routing. Detection, classification and alerting benefit from automation. The reply does not — an automated response under a specific, emotive complaint reads exactly like what it is, and the resulting screenshot travels further than the original comment did."
  - question: "How much does comment management cost?"
    answer: "Quoted per engagement, because the cost follows comment volume, the number of surfaces and the languages involved rather than a fixed tier. A single-market brand with moderate volume and a multi-market brand running continuous paid campaigns are different operations, and pricing them the same would mean one of you subsidising the other."
